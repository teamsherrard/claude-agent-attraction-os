# Style and Craft — part of the aa-logo-design skill

Read in full before designing concepts.

## LOGO STYLE LIBRARY (the leader-brand vocabulary — pick a spread across these)

These are the proven archetypes for a brand that IS a person. Spread your concepts across several so
the member sees genuinely different options, and match each to the brand's feel and finish.

- **Script + block name lockup** — two contrasting faces form the whole logo: a bold caps or refined
  serif surname with a flowing **script** first name (or the reverse), plus a fine tracked descriptor.
  No icon required. Reads personal, confident, boutique. The single most-loved look for a leader whose
  brand is their own name — include one.
- **Constructed monogram + name** — custom interlocking initials (letters share a stroke, nest, or
  overlap) set beside the name. Often two-tone or metallic. Reads designed, premium, ownable.
- **Circle / badge monogram** — the initials inside a thin circle or stamp, with the name beside or
  below. Classic and trusted; keep the ring fine and the letters custom, not a font dropped in a circle.
- **Idea emblem + name** — a clean geometric emblem that stands for the organization's idea or what
  the member is known for: a rising line for growth, interlocking forms for partnership, a compass or
  north mark for guidance, a simple torch or beacon, an open door for "the way in", a peak for the
  standard they hold. Elevate the geometry so it reads as an emblem, never clip-art. Where the
  organization's name is literal (a collective, a pack, a crew, a lab, a studio), the emblem may say it.
- **Geometric mark in a shape** — initials inside a hexagon, shield, or circle. Modern, bold, badge-like.
- **Elegant wordmark** — the name alone in a distinctive face (high-contrast serif for premium, clean
  grotesk for modern), with a small tracked descriptor. Reads confident and timeless.
- **Single lettermark** — one stylized initial as the mark, with the full name set cleanly alongside.

What is NOT in this library by default: the house, the roofline, the key, the map pin, the window —
the listing-brand set. They enter only when the member asks or the organization's name calls for them.

## FINISHES, GRADIENTS & METALLICS (use them with intent)

A logo does not have to be flat. Premium leader brands often use a metallic or gradient finish —
choose it from the brand's feel, and always keep a flat one-colour version that still works (that is
how it prints on a stamp, a sign, or an embroidered shirt). Available finishes:

- **Flat** — one or two solid colours. Cleanest, most modern, most versatile.
- **Two-tone** — two brand colours split across the mark (one letter of a monogram in the accent).
- **Metallic gradient** — gold, rose gold, copper, bronze, or silver as a smooth light→deep gradient so
  it reads as real foil. For premium and classic brands; a soft sheen, never a rainbow.
- **Gold foil / rose-gold / copper / silver-chrome** — full metallic treatment of the mark or
  initials. Pair with deep navy, black, charcoal, forest, or cream grounds.
- **Subtle dimensional** — a faint bevel on a monogram, used sparingly; flat-but-metallic usually
  reads more premium than heavy 3D.
- **Gradient stroke / duotone** — a colour-to-colour gradient along an outline or split fill for
  modern brands.

Rules of thumb: match the finish to the direction; ONE finish system per logo; whatever the finish,
the mark must hold up as a flat one-colour silhouette and stay legible at small sizes.

**COLOUR-ON-DARK RULES — build the dark-ground version like a designer, never like a filter.** A
dark-background version is NOT a colour inversion. Keep the accent metallic exactly as it is (gold is
BUILT for dark grounds) and swap only the dark structural colour (navy, charcoal, black) to white,
cream, or a pale tint of the brand colour. Two hard rules with numbers:
- **Pale metallics DIE on white.** A light gold (around #D4BC8B or lighter) carrying the name or a
  thin mark on a white ground reads washed-out and cheap. On white, deepen the metallic toward its
  shadow tone (the #8C6A2F–#A8843C range) or set it on a dark panel — and save the pale, glowing gold
  for the dark version, where it looks like money.
- **Thin strokes erode on dark.** Light-on-dark marks and type optically thin out and fill in at small
  sizes — draw the dark-ground version's strokes roughly 10–15% thicker (or one weight up) and check
  nothing vanishes at card size. A dark version that is just the same artwork inverted is a failed render.

**DISTINCT FROM THE BROKERAGE — the hue check.** The brief names the brokerage's palette. Before you
commit a lead colour, hold it beside the brokerage's: same hue family at a glance (their navy, your
navy; their orange, your orange) = too close. Shift hue, depth, or temperature until the member's mark
is unmistakably theirs. Distinct does not mean hidden: the brokerage mark still appears where the
member's compliance rules require, as its own quiet chip.

## TYPOGRAPHY & COLOUR CRAFT (where good becomes premium)

The typeface IS the logo for a wordmark and carries half the weight of any monogram — choose it with
intent. Match the type's character to the member's feel:

- **Premium / boutique / calm authority** — a high-contrast "fashion" serif reads expensive and editorial.
- **Modern / clean / systems-minded** — a geometric sans (even strokes, circular forms) reads crisp.
- **Warm / approachable / mentor** — a humanist sans or a soft slab reads friendly and grounded.
- **Established / trustworthy / traditional** — a classic transitional serif reads stable and credible.
- **Bold / high-energy / builder** — a strong display sans reads confident and loud in a good way.

**Script / signature faces (for the two-font name lockup).** The script must be an elegant, real,
legible signature or calligraphic face — flowing but readable, never a stiff "computer cursive". Pair
it with a contrasting partner (bold caps sans or high-contrast serif) so block and script play off
each other. Use the script for ONE word only; never the whole name or the descriptor.

Treat the type like a designer would: pair ONE distinctive face (the name) with ONE quiet supporting
line (the descriptor) in a simple tracked-out sans, so the two never compete. Give the name generous,
even letter-spacing — wide tracking almost always looks more premium than tight.

**The proportions that make a lockup read professionally set (use these numbers):**
- **Descriptor scale:** the descriptor's cap height is 25–35% of the name's cap height, with wide, even
  tracking (roughly 0.2–0.3em of letter-spacing).
- **Mark scale:** a mark ABOVE the name is about 1.5–2.5× the name's cap height; a mark BESIDE the name
  matches the name's full cap-to-baseline height or slightly exceeds it.
- **Script sizing in the combo:** set the script word 1.8–2.5× the block word's cap height so the two
  words feel equally weighted. A tail may underline or overlap the block word only while both stay
  perfectly legible.
- **Centre optically, not mathematically:** a script with a long opening flourish is nudged so the
  WORD looks centred, not its bounding box.

**FONT RULE — only use and name fonts the design tool actually has.** Every font in the family must be
a real, reliably available face (Google Fonts and the tool's own library); never spec a paid or
unavailable font while printing its name. The font name in the hand-off package MUST be the font
actually rendered — and it must cover the brand language's full character set.

**Colour within the mark:** restraint reads premium. Work primarily in one brand colour; introduce a
second only with purpose (a two-tone monogram where one letter carries the accent). The mark must
still hold up perfectly as a single flat colour.
