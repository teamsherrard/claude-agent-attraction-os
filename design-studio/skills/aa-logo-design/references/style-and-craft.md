# Style and Craft — part of the aa-logo-design skill

Read in full before designing concepts.

## LOGO STYLE LIBRARY (the leader-brand vocabulary — pick a spread across these)

These are the proven archetypes for a brand that IS a person. Spread your concepts across several so
the member sees genuinely different options, and match each to the brand's feel and finish.

- **Script + block name lockup** — two contrasting faces form the whole logo: a bold caps or refined
  serif surname with a flowing **script** first name (or the reverse), plus a fine tracked descriptor.
  No icon required. Reads personal, confident, boutique. The single most-loved look for a leader whose
  brand is their own name — include one.
- **Constructed monogram + name** — custom interlocking initials (letters share a stroke, nest, or
  overlap) set beside the name. Often two-tone or metallic. Reads designed, premium, ownable.
- **Circle / badge monogram** — the initials inside a thin circle or stamp, with the name beside or
  below. Classic and trusted; keep the ring fine and the letters custom, not a font dropped in a circle.
- **Idea emblem + name** — a clean geometric emblem that stands for the organization's idea or what
  the member is known for: a rising line for growth, interlocking forms for partnership, a compass or
  north mark for guidance, a simple torch or beacon, a path or a bridge for "the way in", a peak for the
  standard they hold. Elevate the geometry so it reads as an emblem, never clip-art. Where the
  organization's name is literal (a collective, a pack, a crew, a lab, a studio), the emblem may say it.
- **Geometric mark in a shape** — initials inside a hexagon, shield, or circle. Modern, bold, badge-like.
- **Elegant wordmark** — the name alone in a distinctive face (high-contrast serif for premium, clean
  grotesk for modern), with a small tracked descriptor. Reads confident and timeless.
- **Single lettermark** — one stylized initial as the mark, with the full name set cleanly alongside.
- **Illustrated emblem + name** — a creature, a figure, a mask, a crest drawn as a **single-weight line
  illustration** (one stroke weight, vector, no shading, no fill gradients inside the drawing) when the
  organization's idea IS one — a pack, a crew, a guild, a school. The emblem sits above or beside the
  organization's name; a **simplified version** (half the detail, same silhouette) is drawn for the
  40px circle and the favicon and proven there before the concept is shown. Never a cartoon mascot,
  never clip-art, never two weights.
- **"[Organization] by [Name]"** — the organization's name as the hero line (tracked caps or a strong
  display face), "BY [FIRST LAST]" as the descriptor beneath at descriptor scale, an optional emblem
  above. One lockup that says both; a sibling of the member's own version, never a stranger.

What is NEVER in this library: the house, the roofline, the key, the map pin, the window, the skyline,
the "SOLD" rider — the listing-brand set. This is a coaching brand for agents, not a realtor brand for
consumers; when a member asks for one of these, the leader's emblem carries their method instead.

## FINISHES, GRADIENTS & METALLICS (use them with intent)

A logo does not have to be flat. Premium leader brands often use a metallic or gradient finish —
choose it from the brand's feel, and always keep a flat one-colour version that still works (that is
how it reads as a video watermark, a favicon, or a one-colour overlay on a photo). Available finishes:

- **Flat** — one or two solid colours. Cleanest, most modern, most versatile.
- **Two-tone** — two brand colours split across the mark (one letter of a monogram in the accent).
- **Metallic gradient** — gold, rose gold, copper, bronze, or silver as a smooth light→deep gradient so
  it reads as real foil. For premium and classic brands; a soft sheen, never a rainbow.
- **Gold foil / rose-gold / copper / silver-chrome** — full metallic treatment of the mark or
  initials. Pair with deep navy, black, charcoal, forest, or cream grounds.
- **Subtle dimensional** — a faint bevel on a monogram, used sparingly; flat-but-metallic usually
  reads more premium than heavy 3D.
- **Gradient stroke / duotone** — a colour-to-colour gradient along an outline or split fill for
  modern brands.
- **Gradient fill on a bold wordmark** — the brand's two or three hues as ONE warm→cool (or light→deep)
  gradient across a heavy condensed name, for the high-energy coach look. One gradient, one direction,
  never a rainbow; the flat one-colour version always kept.

**Which finish:** the brief's Creative direction names the look (the Design System's house style
library, §12) — the gold-on-black and navy-and-gold looks want foil on the mark or the initials; the
bold gradient look wants the gradient wordmark; the editorial, paper, and brushed looks want flat or
two-tone with the metallic as a thin accent at most; the bright accent look is flat. Match the finish
to the look; never pick one by taste.

Rules of thumb: match the finish to the direction; ONE finish system per logo; whatever the finish,
the mark must hold up as a flat one-colour silhouette and stay legible at small sizes.

**COLOUR-ON-DARK RULES — build the dark-ground version like a designer, never like a filter.** A
dark-background version is NOT a colour inversion. Keep the accent metallic exactly as it is (gold is
BUILT for dark grounds) and swap only the dark structural colour (navy, charcoal, black) to white,
cream, or a pale tint of the brand colour. Two hard rules with numbers:
- **Pale metallics DIE on white.** A light gold (around #D4BC8B or lighter) carrying the name or a
  thin mark on a white ground reads washed-out and cheap. On white, deepen the metallic toward its
  shadow tone (the #8C6A2F–#A8843C range) or set it on a dark panel — and save the pale, glowing gold
  for the dark version, where it looks like money.
- **Thin strokes erode on dark.** Light-on-dark marks and type optically thin out and fill in at small
  sizes — draw the dark-ground version's strokes roughly 10–15% thicker (or one weight up) and check
  nothing vanishes at profile-circle size. A dark version that is just the same artwork inverted is a failed render.

**THE LOGO SYSTEM — the numbers that make the family read as a system.**
- **The 3×2 variations grid:** three tiles across (stacked · horizontal · mark or wordmark alone), two
  rows (over the textured dark ground · over white), equal tile sizes, equal gutters, every mark
  optically centred in its tile, the dark row's texture at real strength (contour lines, brushed
  leather, smoke, marble — whichever the look names) and the white row plain or with the faintest paper
  grain. The grid is the presentation standard for the chosen family and the one the style sheet reuses.
- **Two grounds, one file:** the PNG and SVG files carry no ground, no texture, no sheen — the texture is
  the board's. The reversed version is built per the colour-on-dark rules above, not by inverting.
- **The brokered-by lockup:** member's mark → a 1px hairline divider at the mark's full cap height (or
  the emblem's height) with clear space of at least half the mark height on each side → a tracked
  "BROKERED BY" eyebrow at about 20–25% of the brokerage mark's height, set in the descriptor face →
  the brokerage mark, as supplied, at **no more than 40% of the member's mark height**, one-colour where
  the brokerage allows it. Horizontal only. Built only from the uploaded brokerage file, only when the
  compliance line requires the brokerage mark beside the member's own; shipped as
  `logo-brokered-lockup.png` / `.svg`; never the primary, never in the content header.
- **"BY [NAME]" under an organization name:** descriptor scale (cap height 25–35% of the hero line,
  tracking 0.2–0.3em), centred under the hero line, the word "BY" in the same face and weight as the name.
- **The emblem:** one stroke weight, matched to the heaviest stroke of the type beside it; the full
  drawing holds at the header size, the simplified version (same silhouette, half the interior lines)
  holds at 48px; both share the one finish system.

**DISTINCT FROM THE BROKERAGE — the hue check.** The brief names the brokerage's palette. Before you
commit a lead colour, hold it beside the brokerage's: same hue family at a glance (their navy, your
navy; their orange, your orange) = too close. Shift hue, depth, or temperature until the member's mark
is unmistakably theirs. Distinct does not mean hidden: the brokerage mark still appears where the
member's compliance rules require, as its own quiet chip.

## TYPOGRAPHY & COLOUR CRAFT (where good becomes premium)

The typeface IS the logo for a wordmark and carries half the weight of any monogram — choose it with
intent. Match the type's character to the member's feel:

- **Premium / boutique / calm authority** — a high-contrast "fashion" serif reads expensive and editorial.
- **Modern / clean / systems-minded** — a geometric sans (even strokes, circular forms) reads crisp.
- **Warm / approachable / mentor** — a humanist sans or a soft slab reads friendly and grounded.
- **Established / trustworthy / traditional** — a classic transitional serif reads stable and credible.
- **Bold / high-energy / builder** — a strong display sans reads confident and loud in a good way.

**Script / signature faces (for the two-font name lockup).** The script must be an elegant, real,
legible signature or calligraphic face — flowing but readable, never a stiff "computer cursive". Pair
it with a contrasting partner (bold caps sans or high-contrast serif) so block and script play off
each other. Use the script for ONE word only; never the whole name or the descriptor.

Treat the type like a designer would: pair ONE distinctive face (the name) with ONE quiet supporting
line (the descriptor) in a simple tracked-out sans, so the two never compete. Space the name by what it is — the numbers are in CASTING THE TYPE, below: caps open up, heavy display
faces stay tight, a script is never spaced out.

**The proportions that make a lockup read professionally set (use these numbers):**
- **Descriptor scale:** the descriptor's cap height is 25–35% of the name's cap height, with wide, even
  tracking (roughly 0.2–0.3em of letter-spacing).
- **Mark scale:** a mark ABOVE the name is about 1.5–2.5× the name's cap height; a mark BESIDE the name
  matches the name's full cap-to-baseline height or slightly exceeds it.
- **Script sizing in the combo:** set the script word 1.8–2.5× the block word's cap height so the two
  words feel equally weighted. A tail may underline or overlap the block word only while both stay
  perfectly legible.
- **Centre optically, not mathematically:** a script with a long opening flourish is nudged so the
  WORD looks centred, not its bounding box.

**FONT RULE — only use and name fonts the design tool actually has.** Every font in the family must be
a real, reliably available face (Google Fonts and the tool's own library); never spec a paid or
unavailable font while printing its name. The font name in the hand-off package MUST be the font
actually rendered — and it must cover the brand language's full character set.

**Colour within the mark:** restraint reads premium. Work primarily in one brand colour; introduce a
second only with purpose (a two-tone monogram where one letter carries the accent). The mark must
still hold up perfectly as a single flat colour.

## CONSTRUCTION — how a mark is really built (read before you draw)

A logo drawn as "some shapes that look about right" reads as machine-made at a glance. Build it.

- **The grid.** Draw every mark as vector on a square field — a `0 0 240 240` viewBox, an 8-unit step —
  and keep every point on a step. Few angles (0° · 45° · 60° · 90°), few radii (one, or two in a 1:2
  ratio). Symmetry is exact: draw one half and mirror it, never eyeball both sides.
- **One stroke weight per mark**, taken from the name beside it (the heaviest stroke of the name's face).
  Round ends and corners with a soft face, square ones with a sharp face — the mark and the type should
  look cut by the same tool.
- **A monogram is constructed. Six moves — use ONE, cleanly:**
  1. *Shared stroke* — the two letters own one vertical or one diagonal between them.
  2. *Nested* — the smaller letter sits inside the bowl or the open side of the larger.
  3. *Interlocked* — one letter passes over the other, and the lower one is cut away around it with an
     even gap (about half the stroke), like two links.
  4. *Stacked under one bar* — one letter above the other, joined by a shared horizontal.
  5. *The hidden letter* — the second initial is made by the empty space inside or beside the first.
  6. *One line* — both letters drawn as a single continuous stroke.
  Then the test: is it still clearly both letters, in the right order, at a glance? An unreadable monogram
  is decoration, and two font letters merely standing next to each other are not a monogram at all.
- **A frame is the last thing added and the first thing questioned.** If a ring, a shield or a badge is
  doing the work, the letters inside are not finished. A frame that stays matches the letters' stroke and
  keeps an inner margin of at least one and a half strokes all round.
- **An idea mark is three to seven shapes.** Reduce the idea (a rising line, a north point, a table, a
  bridge, a beacon, a path) until taking one more shape away would lose it. More than seven shapes is an
  illustration, and it will not survive a profile circle.
- **Correct by eye, the way type designers do:** round and pointed shapes reach 2–3% past the flat ones (or
  they look small); a horizontal stroke is drawn about a tenth thinner than a vertical to look the same; a
  mark above a name is centred on the name's visual middle, not on its box.
- **Simple proportions.** The mark sits in a square or a 4:5 field; the widest line of the lockup sets the
  width, and the other lines relate to it simply (the descriptor the full width of the name, or centred at
  two thirds of it).
- **The 40-pixel proof, every time.** Before a direction is kept: render the mark in a 40px circle and at
  16px, in one flat colour, on dark and on light — and LOOK at those renders. Shapes that close up get
  opened; strokes that vanish go one weight heavier; detail that turns to noise is removed. The small
  version may be a simpler drawing of the same idea — it is still the same mark.
- **One flat colour first.** A second colour, a foil or a gradient goes only onto a mark that already works
  as a solid shape.
- **Clean files.** Shapes as paths and simple primitives, no stray points, nothing hidden behind a clipping
  mask, no picture embedded in the vector, a see-through background, and the artwork tight to its own
  bounds — the clear space belongs to the layout, never to the file.

## CASTING THE TYPE — real faces, real numbers

The name's typeface is a casting decision: the same name reads as a different person in a different face.
Cast from faces that really load (every one below is a Google Font); the brief's Type line and its first
reference recipe say which shelf to start on.

| The brief says | Cast the name from | The line under it |
|---|---|---|
| classic · calm authority · editorial | Playfair Display · Cormorant Garamond (600–700) · DM Serif Display · Bodoni Moda · Fraunces · Marcellus (caps) | Montserrat · Josefin Sans · Jost — 500–600, caps |
| classic · established · trusted | Libre Baskerville · EB Garamond · Libre Caslon Display · Spectral · Cinzel (caps only) | Inter · Source Sans 3 — 600, caps |
| modern · clean · systems-minded | Montserrat · Poppins · Plus Jakarta Sans · Manrope · Outfit · Sora · Urbanist · Josefin Sans | the same family — 500–600, caps |
| warm · mentor · approachable | Fraunces · Lora · Nunito Sans · Work Sans · Source Sans 3 · Zilla Slab | Inter · Work Sans — 500–600, caps |
| bold · high energy · builder | Bebas Neue · Anton · Oswald (600–700) · Archivo Black · Barlow Condensed (700–800) · League Spartan · Big Shoulders Display | Montserrat · Barlow — 600–700, caps |
| the script word of a two-face name | elegant: Great Vibes · Allura · Alex Brush · Parisienne · Pinyon Script — signature: Mrs Saint Delafield · Herr Von Muellerhoff — brush: Kaushan Script · Yellowtail — single line: Sacramento | — |

**Setting the name (the numbers):**
- **Caps in a serif or a light sans:** letter-spacing +0.06 to +0.14em — the lighter the weight, the wider.
- **Caps in a heavy geometric sans:** +0.02 to +0.08em. **Heavy condensed caps** (Bebas Neue, Anton): 0 to +0.03em.
- **A name in mixed case:** the face's own spacing, at most −0.01em on a large heavy setting. Never spaced wide.
- **A script word:** 0, always. Spacing a script apart breaks its joins and is the fastest way to look cheap.
- **The line under the name:** caps, +0.2 to +0.3em, weight 500–600 — and when it is centred, nudge it right
  by half its letter-spacing (the spacing is added after the last letter too, so a spaced line sits visibly
  left of centre unless you correct it).
- **Close the gaps a font leaves** in a name set large: the pairs that gape (A V · A W · T o · L T · P a ·
  r a) are tightened by eye until the space between every pair of letters looks equal. Kerning and ligatures
  stay switched on.
- **A thin script dies small.** A hairline signature face is only ever the second voice beside a bold word —
  never the whole logo, and never the version that goes in the profile circle; that surface gets the mark or
  the bold word.
- **Two faces need real contrast** — a serif with a sans, a script with a block, heavy with light. Two similar
  sans faces side by side read as a mistake.
- **Check the name's own letters.** A face with a beautiful R and an awkward K is the wrong face for a
  Katherine. Set the member's actual name before you fall for the font.
